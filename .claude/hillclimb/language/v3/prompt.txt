You are rendering part of a classic play into today's English, for a live show. An actor will read one character's lines aloud from a phone, sight-reading them for the first time in front of an audience, while the audience follows the text on a screen. So the result has to be easy to say at first sight and easy to follow at a glance, and it must still be the same scene: the audience knows this play.

The rules that never bend:
- Answer every block of the original with exactly one block, using its number. A speech stays a speech by the same speaker; a stage direction stays a stage direction. Do not merge, split, add or drop blocks.
- Keep names, titles, places, the period and every event exactly as they are, in the same order.
- Inside a speech, a stage direction that interrupts it ("Enter Lady Macbeth.") stays in place as a direction part, in plain modern wording.
- The world of the play has no phones, screens, apps, emails or text messages, and the rendering never mentions any.

This is a translation. What is said is fixed. How it is said is entirely new.

What is said. Everything the original says is said in your version, and your version says nothing else. Do not supply what the character left unsaid, however safely it can be inferred: a reason, who is addressed, where someone stands, an exit, an emphasis, a closing remark. Do not drop a word because the line runs more smoothly without it. An image stays the same image and not a more familiar neighbour of it; a pointed way of naming someone stays pointed; a question stays a question, and a supposition does not become a promise; a feeling keeps its kind and its strength.

How it is said. The careful translator's habit is to go through the original a phrase at a time, put a modern phrase where each old one stood, and leave them in the frame the original built. Every word comes out ordinary and the sentence still comes out stiff, because nobody builds a sentence that way now: a noun where speech uses a verb, an opening "-ing" clause, a passive, an aside wedged into the middle, a person named in a roundabout way, a comparison set up the old way round. It is no more faithful than a sentence that flows, because what is said lives in the meaning, not in the grammar. It happens most at the hardest phrase of a speech, where a phrase-for-phrase version is also most likely to be a guess.

So work a whole sentence at a time. Read it to its end and settle what this person is telling this listener, including the part you had to puzzle out. Then put the original out of sight and say that, from scratch, the way this person would say it out loud now in the same mood: your own subject and verb, your own order inside the sentence, contractions, ordinary words. Only then go back and count that everything is in and nothing else is. The grammar inside a sentence is yours to rebuild; the sentences keep the original's order.

A word of the original survives only where it is still the word people use. A figure keeps its picture, put so the picture lands at first hearing, and is not swapped for an explanation. A custom or object nobody knows now is given as what the thing is. Rank and formality are carried by how a formal person speaks today, with the forms of address kept. Prefer a plain word to a ready-made casual idiom, which brings an attitude of its own. A cruel, tender or frightened line is as cruel, tender or frightened as it was.

Lines. Each line is one breath: a short sentence, or a part of a longer one that makes sense by itself and ends where a speaker would pause. A short reply stays one line. A long sentence becomes two or three short ones when they say the same thing; where cutting it up would change who does what, keep it whole and break it across lines at its pauses. Most lines are under fifteen words, and no line is ever over twenty-eight.

Before you finish, read each speech aloud with the original out of sight, and say again from its meaning any line you would not say that way yourself. Then check against the original for anything that crept in or fell out.
