You are rendering part of a classic play into today's English, for a live show. An actor will read one character's lines aloud from a phone, sight-reading them for the first time in front of an audience, while the audience follows the text on a screen. So the result has to be easy to say at first sight and easy to follow at a glance, and it must still be the same scene: the audience knows this play and will hold your version against it phrase by phrase.

The rules that never bend:
- Answer every block of the original with exactly one block, using its number. A speech stays a speech by the same speaker; a stage direction stays a stage direction. Do not merge, split, add or drop blocks.
- Keep names, titles, places, the period and every event exactly as they are, in the same order.
- Inside a speech, a stage direction that interrupts it ("Enter Lady Macbeth.") stays in place as a direction part, in plain modern wording.
- The world of the play has no phones, screens, apps, emails or text messages, and the rendering never mentions any.

This is a translation, and a translation has two sides. What is said is fixed. How it is said is entirely new.

What is said. Each phrase of the original has its counterpart in your version, and nothing in your version is without one. Do not supply what the character left unsaid, however safely it can be inferred: a reason, who is being addressed, where someone stands, an exit, a word of emphasis, a closing remark. Do not drop a word because the line runs more smoothly without it. An image stays the same image and not a more familiar neighbour of it; a pointed way of naming someone stays pointed; a question stays a question, and a supposition does not become a promise; a feeling keeps its kind and its strength. When a phrase is not immediately clear, work out what it means in this scene before you write, rather than going by how the words look today.

How it is said. The easy way to keep all of that is to leave the original's words and sentence shapes standing and only tidy the grammar. The result is a line nobody would say: an old word kept because it is still in the dictionary, a figure carried across word for word so that it reads as a riddle, an oath or a courtesy left in its old form, a sentence that still runs in the old order with its clauses stacked up. That is not faithful, it is untranslated, and it is the commonest way a careful rendering fails. So work from the meaning, not the wording. For each phrase, settle what this person is telling this listener, then say exactly that, no more and no less, the way someone would say it out loud now in the same mood: contractions, ordinary words, the sentence in the order speech takes. A word of the original survives only where it is still the word people use. A figure keeps its picture, put so the picture lands at first hearing, and is not swapped for an explanation of what it means. A name for a custom or an object nobody knows now is given as what the thing is. Rank and formality are carried by how a formal person speaks today, with the forms of address kept. Prefer a plain word to a ready-made casual idiom, which brings an attitude of its own.

Each character still sounds like themselves, and the heat stays: a cruel, tender or frightened line is as cruel, tender or frightened as it was.

Lines. Each line is one breath and one thought, something the actor takes in at a glance and delivers. A short reply stays one line. A long sentence does not come across as one long sentence: make it two or three short ones, each on its own line, in the same order with everything still in them. Most lines are under fifteen words, and none needs a second look to find its shape.

Before you finish, test each speech twice. First with the original out of sight: read your lines aloud, and wherever you can hear the old sentence underneath, say it again as speech. Then against the original: take out anything that crept in and put back anything that fell out.
