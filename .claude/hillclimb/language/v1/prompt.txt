You are rendering part of a classic play into today's English, for a live show. An actor will read one character's lines aloud from a phone, sight-reading them for the first time in front of an audience, while the audience follows the text on a screen. So the result has to be easy to say at first sight and easy to follow at a glance, and it must still be the same scene: the audience knows this play and should recognise it.

Change the language, never the story:
- Keep every event, every piece of information, every image that matters, and the order of everything. Keep names, places and the period exactly as they are.
- Answer every block of the original with exactly one block, using its number. A speech stays a speech by the same speaker; a stage direction stays a stage direction. Do not merge, split, add or drop blocks.
- Write plain, natural, contemporary English that a person would actually say. It should not sound like a summary, a study guide, or slang. Keep the heat of the original: when a line is cruel, tender or frightened, the modern line is too.
- Give each character their own voice, and keep it steady through the scene.
- Break each speech into short lines of one or two sentences. Each line is one breath: something the actor can take in at a glance and deliver. A long speech becomes several lines; a short reply stays one line.
- Inside a speech, a stage direction that interrupts it ("Enter Lady Macbeth.") stays in place as a direction part, in plain modern wording.
- The world of the play has no phones, screens, apps, emails or text messages, and the rendering never mentions any.

Stay as close to the original as a translator would. People who know the play will hold your version against it phrase by phrase, and what they notice is not the overall sense, which you will get right, but the small places where your line says something the original does not. A rendering with one of these in it reads as worse than a plainer one with none. They come in a few kinds:

- Something added. The original leaves a thing unsaid and the rendering supplies it: what a claim is a claim to, why someone acted, who is being spoken to, where a person is standing, an extra exit or gesture in a direction, an intensifier or a closing remark for emphasis. If the character did not say it, the modern line does not say it either, however safely it can be inferred. Every phrase you write should be traceable to a phrase in the original.
- Something swapped. An image is replaced by a neighbouring one that feels more vivid or more familiar, a trade or rank or object by a different one, a pointed way of naming someone by the neutral name, a title or form of address by a looser one. Translate the thing that is there. The same holds for the shape of the thought: a question stays a question, a supposition stays a supposition and does not become a promise or a prediction, a comparison keeps both its halves, and whoever acts in the original still acts.
- Something lost. A word carrying force or a second item in a pair is quietly dropped because the line reads more smoothly without it. If it was said, it is said.
- A feeling moved. Contempt is not indifference, sullenness is not sadness, a formal courtesy is not a friendly one. Find the modern word for the exact feeling and its exact strength, and prefer an ordinary word to a ready-made casual idiom, which usually carries a different attitude from the one in the line.
- A hard phrase guessed. The passages are full of words that have changed meaning and of syntax that folds back on itself. When a phrase is not immediately clear, work out what it means in this scene, who or what each word refers to, before writing; do not render it from how the words look today. Where two readings remain possible, choose wording that holds for both rather than committing to one.

Closeness applies to length as well. When the original packs a thought into a few words, the modern line is short too: give the figure itself in plain words and let it land, rather than unpacking it into an explanation of what it means. The explanation is longer, harder to say and flatter. When the original sentence is long, do not carry it over as one modern sentence with clauses stacked up; make it two or three short sentences, each on its own line, with everything still in them and in the same order. A line the actor would have to read twice to find its shape is not finished. Most lines should come in well under twenty words.

Being close does not mean being stiff. Word order, grammar and vocabulary are all modern; it is the content that is fixed. Once a speech is drafted, read it against the original once more and take out whatever is yours, then put back whatever is missing.
